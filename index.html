A²-Bench

Benchmarking agent interactions
across boundaries.

A²-Bench evaluates AI agents that must collaborate while operating in separate environments.Each agent has its own data, tools, and instructions and represents a person or organization. The benchmark measures both task completion and whether agents adhere to the requirements of the people or organizations they represent.

TLDR

What it tests. A²-Bench evaluates agents working in separate environments, each with private files, tools, and instructions. It measures task success and fidelity: whether agents follow their principals’ instructions, protect their interests, and respect confidentiality. A principal is the person or organization an agent represents.

Tasks and scenarios. The benchmark currently includes about 500 scenarios, with more expected to be added over time, spanning eight domains: legal review, customer relationship management (CRM), risk management, evolving enterprise (SQL data requests), software engineering, security incident response, commerce, and contract negotiation. Agents write legal memos, resolve customer cases, answer data requests, approve software releases, and negotiate contracts. They may cooperate on shared goals, face conflicting incentives such as concealing incident evidence, or compete as buyers and sellers with private budgets and prices.

Failures and findings. Agents may fail to request or use peer-held information, submit before replies arrive, accept claims without checking evidence, or disclose private negotiating limits. Even successful task completion can involve violations of a principal’s instructions or interests. Evaluation must therefore assess both results and how agents obtain, verify and share information.

WHY EVALUATE AGENT COLLABORATION?

Agents need to exchange information
across separate workspaces.

Agents may need data or capabilities held by other agents. They must request the necessary information, verify supporting evidence, and act in accordance with their users’ instructions. For example, to schedule a meeting, two personal agents exchange availability without accessing each other’s private calendars. They verify that the proposed time satisfies both users’ constraints while keeping other calendar details confidential. Similar interactions arise in legal reviews, software releases, and purchases. Each agent represents a principal—a person or organization whose interests may align with or conflict with those of other principals.

THE RESEARCH QUESTIONCan agents complete the assigned task and follow their own principals’ instructions and confidentiality requirements?

An Illustrative Example: Two Agents, One Legal Review

Morgan and Sam are two AI agents helping a company review a law firm’s proposed engagement letter—the contract that sets the firm’s fees and terms. Their goal is to identify terms that conflict with the company’s guidelines and prepare a memo explaining what needs to change before signing.

Morgan prepares the review

Morgan has the company’s guidelines, a staffing plan and a vendor proposal. Morgan is responsible for writing and submitting the final memo.

Sam holds the missing documents

Sam is the communications analyst. Sam’s files contain the engagement letter and emails about a possible conflict of interest.

Why they need to collaborate: Morgan cannot access Sam’s data directly. To assess the letter’s terms and the conflict-of-interest evidence, Morgan needs to request that information from Sam through messages.

How the memo is scored. The benchmark evaluates the final memo against 45 legal-review checks. For example, checks ask whether the memo identifies fees above the company’s limits, flags the conflict of interest and recommends changes. The task score is the percentage of checks passed. A higher score means the memo meets more of the task’s requirements.

We ran this task six times. The animation below compares two of those runs: one where Morgan requests Sam’s evidence and one where Morgan does not.

Morgan sends Sam a request before finishing the memo. Sam replies with the letter’s key terms and the conflict emails. Morgan uses that evidence to identify an unresolved conflict of interest and advises against signing as drafted.

EVALUATION

Task completion and adherence to instructions

A²-Bench evaluates the completed task and each agent’s conduct separately. It checks whether the result meets the scenario’s requirements and whether each agent follows its principal’s instructions, including confidentiality rules. It also records elapsed time, token use and cost when the harness reports it.

01

Task success

Did the deliverable or final environment pass the scenario’s checks?

0–100Task score

Scenario-specific rubrics, deterministic checks, or environment-state checks assess the outcome.

02

Fidelity to the principal

Did each agent follow its principal’s instructions and confidentiality requirements?

1–10Violation severity · 1 = none, 10 = severe

An evaluator compares each agent’s messages with its private instructions and rates violations from 1 to 10. Automated checks also detect disclosures of specified private values and coordination that violates market rules.

FINDINGS

Six findings from the recorded runs

The findings compare task outcomes with how agents request information, check evidence, wait for replies, and handle confidential information.

01

Task wording affects whether agents contact peers.

+

All agents had a directory of peer names and roles. In the legal tasks, message rates varied with how many peer-held documents the task explicitly named.

02

Agents report documents as missing without requesting them

+

Agents described peer-held documents as unavailable without contacting their owners. Even after sending a message, an entry agent could file before the reply arrived.

1% → 25%

Among legal runs with messages, filing with a request outstanding rose from 1% with two agents to 25% with five.

03

Access restrictions limit independent verification

+

Agents often accepted peer responses without checking the source. In one web lookup, the only peer with internet access supplied the benchmark’s reference answer, but the assigned agent rejected it because it could not inspect the downloaded pages.

In the checkpoint-release example, the reviewer tested the trainer’s responses against private reference values before approving the release.

Example transcript ↗
04

Task completion can coincide with confidentiality or rule violations

+

Agents completed tasks while disclosing private limits or coordinating against market rules. A privacy check on the deliverable can miss what was shared in conversation.

22% vs 1%

In CRM runs, an exact private value appeared in inter-agent messages in 22% of runs, but in filed documents in only 1%.

Market transcript ↗
05

Legal scores grade the entry agent’s deliverable

+

Legal scores varied more across configurations with different entry-agent harnesses than across configurations that changed only the peers’ harnesses. The rubric grades the entry agent’s document, so it captures each peer’s contribution only through that document.

Source run records ↗
06

Message format had limited effects in the tested configurations

+

The paired comparison tested an A2A message wrapper against plain text in three domains. It found similar aggregate scores, elapsed times and disclosure ratings. This result concerns the wrapper used in these experiments; it does not evaluate every feature an A2A implementation could support.

A²-Failures: 20 failure modes in four categories

A²-Failures classifies recurring errors in discovery, grounding, verification and conduct. Each definition refers to the agent’s own instructions and the rules of the scenario. Select a category and failure mode to read its definition.

Discovery failures include silent entry agents, wording-driven discovery, undiscovered capability, delegation without integration, and scope distortion.

DISCOVERY & DELEGATION

Identify which agent holds the required information

A required document or capability may belong to another agent. The assigned agent needs to identify the relevant peer, request the resource and use the response in its final work.

Complete taxonomy in the paper ↗

Implications for agent design and evaluation

The observed failures suggest changes to information requests, evidence handling, confidentiality checks and evaluation. These are design recommendations; the experiments do not establish their effectiveness.

Agents & harnesses

Identify relevant peers, request missing information, incorporate their replies and check unresolved requests before submitting the result.

Protocols

Provide a way to record evidence sources, disclosure permissions, available capabilities and unresolved requests.

Evaluation

Include checks that require information held by peers. Evaluate each agent’s conduct against its own instructions as well as scoring the final outcome.
