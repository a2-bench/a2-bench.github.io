Benchmarking agent interactions

AI agents increasingly work for different people and organizations. They have separate workspaces, private information, and interests that may conflict. A²-Bench tests what happens when they need each other to get the job done.

WHY THIS BENCHMARK

Agent collaboration changes
when there is a boundary.

Most benchmarks evaluate one agent, or a team working for one principal—the person or organization it represents—in a shared environment. That misses a different problem: your agent working with someone else’s agent.

Think of two personal agents scheduling a meeting. Each knows its owner’s calendar and confidentiality rules. Neither can open the other’s laptop. They must discover what the other can do, communicate, and decide what to trust.

The same issue appears in legal work, enterprise workflows, and negotiations. Access to another agent does not guarantee that the agent will ask for what it needs—or protect what should stay private.

THE RESEARCH QUESTIONCan agents complete the task and serve their own principals across these boundaries?

EVALUATION

Task success is only half the picture.

Across these settings, a shared outcome does not imply shared interests. A²-Bench asks two questions: did the task succeed, and did each agent serve its own principal? It also records wall time, tokens, and cost where reported by the harness.

01

Task success

Did the deliverable or final environment pass the scenario’s checks?

0–100Task score

Scenario-specific rubrics, deterministic checks, or environment-state checks assess the outcome.

02

Fidelity to the principal

Did each agent serve the person or organization it represents?

1–10Violation severity · 1 = none, 10 = severe

An interaction judge reads each agent’s messages against its private brief. Deterministic probes detect private-value disclosures and market collusion.

Among runs in four domains with both measures, 22% of those scoring 90 or more had a fidelity violation rated 7 or higher. Most were disclosures of private negotiating terms.

WHAT THE RUNS REVEAL

The failures live in the interactions.

The conversations make the problem visible. Across recorded runs, the final answer can hide whether an agent found the right peer, checked a claim, waited for a reply, or shared something it should have kept private. Six findings describe these patterns.

  • Discovery is driven by task wording.A peer directory alone does not ensure agents reach out.
  • Claims travel more easily than evidence.Separate sandboxes make independent checking difficult.
  • Task success can conceal a fidelity failure.Messages can reveal what the final deliverable misses.

02

“Not provided” can mean “not asked.”

+

Agents described peer-held documents as unavailable without contacting their owners. Even after sending a message, an entry agent could file before the reply arrived.

1% → 25%

Among legal runs with messages, filing with a request outstanding rose from 1% with two agents to 25% with five.

Finding and evidence ↗Example transcript ↗
03

Words cross the boundary; evidence doesn’t.

+

Agents mostly took peers at their word. When they did try to check, another sandbox’s evidence could be inaccessible. In one web lookup, the only peer with internet access was correct, but its answer was overruled.

Verification worked where the scenario’s mechanism required it—for example, fresh fingerprint probes before a checkpoint release.

Finding and evidence ↗Example transcript ↗
04

Success is not fidelity.

+

Agents completed tasks while disclosing private limits or coordinating against market rules. A privacy check on the deliverable can miss what was shared in conversation.

22% vs 1%

In CRM runs, an exact private value appeared in inter-agent messages in 22% of runs, but in filed documents in only 1%.

Finding and evidence ↗Market transcript ↗
05

The score mostly credits the entry agent.

+

Changing the legal entry agent’s harness moved task scores much more than changing its peers’ harnesses. Because the entry agent’s deliverable is graded, failures to obtain or integrate peer evidence can remain under-penalized.

Finding and evidence ↗
06

A message envelope does not solve meaning.

+

In paired configurations, wrapping messages in the A2A envelope rather than plain text did not materially change task scores, wall time, or disclosure. The envelope carries routing and identity, but does not establish provenance, privacy, capabilities, or open-request status.

A²-Failures: a taxonomy of what goes wrong.

A²-Failures gives these recurring problems a common vocabulary: twenty failure modes in four categories. Each is defined against an agent’s own principal and the scenario’s rules.

Discovery failures include silent entry agents, wording-driven discovery, undiscovered capability, delegation without integration, and scope distortion.

DISCOVERY & DELEGATION

Finding out what a peer holds.

A peer can have exactly what the task needs, yet never be contacted. The directory provides a starting point; the agent still has to discover and use the resource.

Read the complete taxonomy ↗

What should change?

The findings and taxonomy point to concrete changes in how agents collaborate, how protocols support them, and how their work is evaluated.

Agents & harnesses

Discover what peers hold and can do. State what is missing, integrate replies, and track requests before filing.

Protocols

Carry provenance, privacy constraints, capability information, and request status—not just routing.

Evaluation

Require peer evidence to pass relevant checks. Evaluate each agent against its own principal, alongside the final outcome.
