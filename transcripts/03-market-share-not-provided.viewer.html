Methodology</u>** **<u>III.A. Data Sources</u>** FTC Staff relied on three primary categories of data in computing the market shares and concentration indices presented in this analysis. **First**, Staff utilized data published by the U.S. Census Bureau for the packaging industry, specifically the Annual Survey of Manufactures and the Economic Census data classified under NAICS 322211 (Corrugated and Solid Fiber Box Manufacturing) and NAICS 322212 (Folding Paperboard Box Manufacturing). Census data provides nationally aggregated revenue, employment, and shipment data for establishments classified under these codes and serves as a benchmark for validating the firm-level data obtained through compulsory process. **Second**, Staff obtained firm-specific revenue, production volume, capacity, and customer data from the twenty largest industry participants by revenue, including both the merging parties and eighteen non-party firms, through subpoena responses obtained pursuant to the Commission's Second Request and separate compulsory process orders. These subpoena responses provided the granular, firm-level data necessary to compute individual market shares for all significant competitors. **Third**, Staff reviewed proprietary industry data maintained by the Packaging Association of America ("PAA"), a trade association that conducts annual surveys of its members covering production volumes, revenues, and capacity utilization rates. The PAA survey data, while not independently verified, provides a useful cross-check against Census and subpoena data. **Calendar Year 2023.** All market share calculations in this analysis use calendar year 2023 data (i.e., January 1 through December 31, 2023) for all firms. Where a firm operates on a non-calendar fiscal year, FTC Staff adjusted the reported figures to a calendar year basis using quarterly revenue data obtained through subpoena responses. **Delivered Pricing.** All revenue figures in this analysis are measured on a delivered-price basis — that is, inclusive of freight and logistics costs — reflecting the prices actually paid by customers. Staff considers delivered pricing to be the appropriate measure of competitive conditions because customers compare competing offers on a delivered basis, and freight costs represent a real and material component of the competitive offering. In the corrugated packaging industry, freight typically adds between 3% and 8% to product prices depending on shipping distance, plant location, and product density. The differential impact of including freight may be non-uniform across firms with different geographic footprints; for example, a firm shipping from the Pacific Northwest to customers in the Midwest or Southeast will incur higher freight costs than a firm with plants proximate to those customers. The delivered-pricing methodology captures these competitive differences. This approach differs from FOB plant pricing, which excludes freight and thus may fail to reflect the true cost to customers of sourcing from geographically distant suppliers. By contrast, the Parties' expert, Dr. Brinkerhoff, relies primarily on Freedonia Group industry reports as the basis for his total market size estimates, supplemented by CPS's and Ridgeway's internal sales data and publicly available SEC filings. Dr. Brinkerhoff uses FOB plant pricing for his revenue calculations. He also uses CPS's fiscal year ending September 30, 2023, rather than the calendar year, which introduces a three-month timing discrepancy. **Sheet Feeder Exclusion.** As introduced in Section II.B, the FTC excludes approximately \$6.5 billion in corrugated sheet sales to independent converters (sheet feeders) from the relevant corrugated packaging market. The rationale for this exclusion is as follows: corrugated sheets sold to independent converters are an upstream intermediate product. These sheet feeders purchase corrugated sheets and convert them into finished containers, which they then sell to end-use customers. The FTC's market definition focuses on the product as experienced by end-use customers — food and beverage companies, consumer goods manufacturers, and industrial users who purchase finished packaging. At the point of competitive interaction between suppliers and packaging purchasers, it is the finished container, not the intermediate sheet, that is the relevant product. Including sheet feeder sales would overstate the total addressable market by approximately \$6.5 billion and would dilute the market shares of vertically integrated manufacturers such as CPS and Pinnacle Paper Group, whose production is disproportionately oriented toward finished containers rather than sheet sales. **<u>III.B. Revenue Attribution Methodology</u>** Revenue is attributed to the firm that is the **seller of record** in the transaction with the end-use customer. Where a firm subcontracts all or a portion of production through tolling or contract manufacturing arrangements, the revenue is attributed to the contracting firm that maintains the customer relationship and invoices the customer, not to the toll manufacturer that physically produces the goods. This attribution principle is relevant to the treatment of the Brentwood Foods Corp. tolling arrangement. Ridgeway holds a multi-year supply agreement with Brentwood Foods Corp. for the supply of folding cartons, with an annual contract value of approximately \$140 million. Under a separate tolling agreement dated June 2021, CPS manufactures approximately 60% of the folding carton volume under the Brentwood Foods contract at CPS's Henderson, Kentucky manufacturing plant. CPS ships the manufactured product to Ridgeway's distribution center, and Ridgeway invoices Brentwood Foods for the final delivered product. In accordance with the seller-of-record principle, the FTC attributes the full \$140 million in Brentwood Foods contract revenue to Ridgeway. Staff notes that the Parties' expert attributes this \$140 million to CPS as the manufacturer, which the FTC considers inappropriate because it does not reflect the competitive reality. Brentwood Foods Corp.'s purchasing relationship is with Ridgeway. CPS is merely a production subcontractor under the tolling arrangement and does not hold any direct commercial relationship with Brentwood Foods for this product. From the customer's perspective — the vantage point from which competitive significance should be assessed — Ridgeway is the supplier. This attribution difference causes a shift of approximately one percentage point in the individual market shares reported for Market 2 (folding cartons). Under the FTC's methodology, CPS holds a 17.0% share and Ridgeway holds a 4.0% share. Under the expert's methodology, CPS holds an 18.0% share and Ridgeway holds a 3.0% share. HHI Calculation Methodology</u>** The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index is the standard measure of market concentration employed in merger analysis by the federal antitrust agencies. HHI is computed as the sum of the squares of each firm's market share, expressed as a percentage. For a market with *n* firms, HHI equals the sum of (S*i)² for each firm \*i\*, where S*i is the firm's market share as a percentage. Pre-merger HHI uses each firm's individual share; post-merger HHI combines the merging parties' shares into a single entity. The merger-induced change in HHI, commonly referred to as the "delta," equals two times the product of the merging firms' respective shares: Δ = 2 × S₁ × S₂. This identity follows from the algebraic expansion of the squared combined share. Under the 2023 Merger Guidelines jointly issued by the Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice, a merger is presumed likely to substantially lessen competition when two conditions are met: (1) the post-merger HHI exceeds 1,800; and (2) the merger increases the HHI by more than 100 points. Where both thresholds are exceeded, the merger gives rise to a structural presumption of illegality, which can be rebutted by the merging parties through evidence of entry, efficiencies, or other factors, but which shifts the burden of production to the respondents. FTC Staff computes HHI using the individual market shares of all firms identified through subpoena responses and Census data, rather than employing a "fringe firm" assumption for the residual "all others" category. Staff has obtained individual firm revenue data for firms accounting for approximately 90% to 95% of each relevant market. The remaining 5% to 10% of each market consists of very small firms whose individual squared shares contribute negligibly to the overall HHI. Staff's approach of using individual firm data provides a more accurate HHI calculation than the approach used by Dr. Brinkerhoff, who aggregates smaller firms into an "all others" category and applies an undisclosed fringe firm assumption for HHI calculation purposes. **<u>IV. Market 4 — Food-Grade Corrugated Packaging (National)</u>** **<u>VII.A. Market Definition</u>** Market 4 is defined as all corrugated packaging products that meet the Food and Drug Administration's food-contact safety standards under 21 CFR Parts 174 through 178, sold in the United States. The relevant geographic market is the United States as a whole. This market is a sub-segment of Market 1 (corrugated packaging), defined by the regulatory and functional requirements applicable to packaging that comes into direct or indirect contact with food products. The basis for treating food-grade corrugated packaging as a separate relevant market rests on three principal considerations. **Supply-side substitutability barriers.** Manufacturing food-grade corrugated packaging requires the use of FDA-compliant raw materials, including food-safe inks, adhesives, coatings, and liner grades. Producers must maintain segregated production lines or implement rigorous changeover and cleaning protocols to prevent contamination. Quality assurance requirements include lot traceability systems, contamination testing regimens, and third-party certifications such as those administered by the Safe Quality Food Institute or the British Retail Consortium. FDA facility inspections add an additional layer of regulatory compliance. These requirements constitute meaningful barriers to supply-side substitution from non-food-grade corrugated producers, who would need to invest in equipment, raw material sourcing, employee training, certification processes, and quality systems before they could compete for food-grade contracts. **Demand-side characteristics.** Food and beverage companies require packaging that meets food-contact safety standards as a non-negotiable procurement criterion driven by federal regulation, brand protection, and consumer safety. These customers cannot substitute non-food-grade corrugated packaging. The price premium for food-grade corrugated products — typically 8% to 15% above standard corrugated — reflects the specialized manufacturing requirements and regulatory compliance costs. A hypothetical monopolist of food-grade corrugated packaging could profitably impose a small but significant and non-transitory increase in price because customers have no viable alternative. **Limited number of qualified suppliers.** Only a subset of corrugated packaging manufacturers maintain the certifications, raw material sourcing relationships, and production protocols necessary to serve the food-grade segment. This results in a more concentrated competitive structure than the broader corrugated market. Genesis and Commercial Rationale</u>** In 2020, Ridgeway won a competitive bid process to become Brentwood Foods' primary folding carton supplier, displacing the incumbent supplier. The resulting Brentwood Foods supply contract, which was executed in late 2020 and commenced in early 2021, committed Ridgeway to supply approximately \$140 million annually in folding carton products for Brentwood Foods' consumer food packaging lines. However, at the time of contract award, Ridgeway lacked sufficient folding carton production capacity to fulfill the full volume commitment. Ridgeway's existing folding carton facilities in the greater Akron, Ohio area could handle approximately 40% of the required annual volume. Rather than invest in new production lines — an investment that would have required significant capital expenditure and an estimated eighteen- to twenty-four-month lead time for equipment procurement, installation, and commissioning — Ridgeway elected to enter into a tolling arrangement with CPS. Under this arrangement, CPS would manufacture the remaining approximately 60% of the contracted volume at CPS's Henderson, Kentucky plant, which had available folding carton production capacity. The Agreement was executed on June 15, 2021, following several months of negotiation between the parties. Under the tolling arrangement, Ridgeway retains the full commercial relationship with Brentwood Foods, including pricing negotiations, order management, quality assurance oversight, and customer service. CPS's role is purely as a contract manufacturer. CPS produces folding cartons to Ridgeway's specifications, using Ridgeway-approved raw materials and Ridgeway-supplied printing dies and artwork, and has no direct contractual relationship with Brentwood Foods. Brentwood Foods is not informed of CPS's involvement, and all product delivered to Brentwood Foods bears Ridgeway branding and packaging identifiers. This commercial structure is significant because it establishes that CPS performs the physical manufacturing function for the majority of the volume, while Ridgeway holds the commercial contract, manages the customer relationship, bears the customer credit risk, and earns the finished-goods margin on the full \$140 million in annual sales to Brentwood Foods. **<u>IV. Key Terms of the Tolling Agreement</u>** **<u>IV.A. Effective Date and Term</u>** The Agreement has an effective date of June 15, 2021. The initial term is five years, running through June 15, 2026. Following the expiration of the initial term, the Agreement automatically renews for successive one-year periods unless either party provides written notice of non-renewal at least twelve months prior to the end of the then-current term. Early termination is permitted only under two circumstances: (a) upon material breach by either party, subject to a ninety-day cure period following written notice of breach; or (b) if the underlying Brentwood Foods supply agreement between Ridgeway and Brentwood Foods is terminated or not renewed for any reason. The Agreement does not contain a termination-for-convenience provision. It should be noted that the Agreement will remain in effect through the anticipated closing of the CPS-Ridgeway merger transaction, which raises integration planning considerations that are outside the scope of this memorandum but should be addressed separately. **<u>IV.B. Volume Commitments and Production Allocation</u>** Under the Agreement, Ridgeway commits to direct approximately 60% of the total Brentwood Foods folding carton volume to CPS's Henderson, Kentucky plant for toll manufacturing. The remaining approximately 40% of the volume is manufactured by Ridgeway at its own folding carton production facilities in the greater Akron, Ohio area. Based on the \$140 million annual contract value of the Ridgeway-Brentwood Foods supply agreement, the tolling volume represents approximately \$84 million in production value at CPS's Henderson plant (60% × \$140 million), with the remaining approximately \$56 million produced by Ridgeway directly (40% × \$140 million). These figures represent approximate production value allocations and do not correspond precisely to the invoiced amounts between the parties, which are governed by the cost-plus pricing structure described in Section IV.C below. Volume may fluctuate quarter-to-quarter based on Brentwood Foods' order patterns and seasonal demand. However, the Agreement specifies annual minimum and maximum allocation bands: a minimum of 55% of total Brentwood Foods volume must be directed to CPS, and a maximum of 65% may be directed to CPS. CPS is required to maintain dedicated production capacity at the Henderson, Kentucky plant sufficient to meet the committed volumes at the upper end of the allocation band. The 60/40 production split is critical to the market share attribution analysis discussed in Section V below, because the majority of the physical manufacturing occurs at CPS's facility, even though Ridgeway manages the entirety of the commercial relationship with Brentwood Foods and produces the remaining 40% at its own plants. **<u>IV.C. Pricing Terms</u>** CPS invoices Ridgeway on a cost-plus basis. The tolling fee charged by CPS to Ridgeway consists of three components: > \(a\) **Direct materials cost** — Paperboard, inks, coatings, and other consumable materials are passed through to Ridgeway at CPS's actual cost, without markup. > > \(b\) **Conversion cost** — A fixed per-unit conversion fee covering labor, energy, and overhead allocated to the Henderson plant production lines dedicated to Brentwood Foods volume. The per-unit conversion fee is adjusted annually based on Producer Price Index (PPI) escalators, specifically the PPI for Converted Paper and Paperboard Products (Series ID WPU0915). > > \(c\) **Margin component** — A fixed percentage markup on the conversion cost component, providing CPS's contractual profit on the tolling services. The specific margin percentage is commercially sensitive and is redacted in the version of the Agreement produced to the FTC pursuant to a confidentiality stipulation. Ridgeway, in turn, invoices Brentwood Foods under the supply agreement at a delivered price that includes Ridgeway's own margin above its combined cost base (i.e., CPS tolling fees for the 60% CPS-manufactured volume, Ridgeway's own production costs for the 40% Ridgeway-manufactured volume, plus logistics and distribution costs for all volume). The pricing structure means that CPS earns tolling services revenue from Ridgeway, while Ridgeway earns finished-goods revenue from Brentwood Foods. The economic substance of the revenue recognized by each party is materially different depending on which party's perspective is adopted. Critically, the total value CPS invoices Ridgeway under the tolling arrangement annually is approximately \$84 million, reflecting cost-plus pricing on 60% of the \$140 million total volume. CPS does not receive \$140 million — it receives tolling fees of approximately \$84 million. The \$140 million figure represents the gross revenue Ridgeway receives from Brentwood Foods for the full scope of folding carton deliveries. This distinction has significant implications for the market share analysis, as discussed in Section V below, because Dr. Brinkerhoff's expert report attributes the full \$140 million to CPS, even though CPS's actual invoiced revenue from the arrangement is approximately \$84 million and CPS manufactures only 60% of the physical volume. **<u>IV.D. Invoicing and Payment Structure</u>** The invoicing flow under the tolling arrangement proceeds as follows: **CPS → Ridgeway:** CPS invoices Ridgeway on a monthly basis for all tolling services rendered during the prior calendar month. Payment terms are Net 45 days from the invoice date. CPS's invoices are classified internally under CPS's "Contract Manufacturing / Tolling Services" revenue category within the Folding Cartons product line. **Ridgeway → Brentwood Foods:** Ridgeway invoices Brentwood Foods directly for all folding carton deliveries, including both CPS-produced and Ridgeway-produced product. Payment terms are governed by the separate Ridgeway-Brentwood Foods supply agreement. All invoices to Brentwood Foods bear Ridgeway's name and branding and are issued from Ridgeway's billing system. Brentwood Foods has no visibility into the tolling arrangement or CPS's role in manufacturing a portion of the product. This invoicing structure has important implications for industry data reporting. In standard industry data sources — including Census Bureau data collected under NAICS 322212 (Folding Paperboard Box Manufacturing), Packaging Association of America trade data, and Freedonia Group market studies — the \$140 million in Brentwood Foods sales would be reported by Ridgeway as its own revenue, because Ridgeway is the invoicing entity. CPS would separately report its approximately \$84 million in tolling fee revenue from Ridgeway. This creates a potential double-counting risk if both figures are included without adjustment, and the question of which figure to use for market share purposes depends entirely on the attribution methodology adopted. **<u>IV.E. Production Specifications and Quality Control</u>** CPS manufactures the folding cartons at the Henderson, Kentucky plant to specifications provided by Ridgeway. Ridgeway specifies the substrates to be used, including solid bleached sulfate (SBS) and coated unbleached kraft (CUK) paperboard, as well as the specific grades and weights of each substrate. Ridgeway provides all printing dies, artwork files, and brand-specific packaging designs to CPS at Ridgeway's expense. CPS is prohibited from using Ridgeway's dies or artwork for any purpose other than the production of Brentwood Foods folding cartons under the Agreement. CPS is responsible for production quality at the Henderson plant, including compliance with all applicable food-contact packaging regulations. However, Ridgeway retains final quality approval authority and conducts periodic audits of the Henderson plant production lines, typically on a quarterly basis. Non-conforming product is CPS's financial responsibility, with all scrap and rework costs borne by CPS. Ridgeway has the right to reject non-conforming product prior to shipment. **<u>IV.F. Logistics and Delivery</u>** Finished folding carton products manufactured at CPS's Henderson, Kentucky plant are shipped FOB Henderson to Ridgeway's regional distribution center located in Medina, Ohio, or to a designated third-party warehouse facility. Ridgeway is responsible for all inbound freight from the Henderson plant and for final delivery to Brentwood Foods' production and distribution facilities. Title and risk of loss transfer from CPS to Ridgeway upon shipment from the Henderson plant loading dock. From that point forward, the product is Ridgeway's property and Ridgeway bears all risk associated with transportation, warehousing, and delivery. This delivery structure reinforces Ridgeway's role as the seller of record — Ridgeway takes physical and legal possession of the product, warehouses it, and delivers it to Brentwood Foods under its own logistics operations and transportation contracts. **<u>IV.G. Exclusivity and Non-Compete</u>** The Agreement contains mutual restrictive covenants. CPS agrees not to solicit Brentwood Foods directly for folding carton business during the term of the Agreement and for a period of eighteen months following the effective date of termination. This non-solicitation obligation applies to CPS and all of its subsidiaries and affiliates. Ridgeway, in turn, agrees not to source more than 10% of the Brentwood Foods folding carton volume from any toll manufacturer other than CPS during the term. Ridgeway retains the right to produce up to 100% of the volume at its own facilities if it develops sufficient internal capacity, but any outsourced production beyond the 10% threshold must be placed with CPS. These provisions reinforce the commercial reality that Ridgeway "owns" the Brentwood Foods customer relationship. The non-solicitation clause, in particular, represents CPS's contractual acknowledgment that the Brentwood Foods account belongs to Ridgeway, and that CPS's role is limited to toll manufacturing in support of Ridgeway's supply obligations. **<u>V. Revenue Attribution Analysis — Implications for Market Share Calculations</u>** **<u>V.A. Expert's Attribution Methodology (Dr. Brinkerhoff)</u>** Dr. Brinkerhoff's Expert Report (dated March 14, 2025) attributes the full \$140 million in Brentwood Foods folding carton revenue to CPS in Market B (Folding Cartons — National). His stated rationale is that CPS is the "manufacturer of record" for approximately 60% of the volume, and that the tolling arrangement makes CPS the economic producer of the Brentwood Foods folding carton output. The expert appears to treat the entire \$140 million as CPS revenue on the theory that CPS's manufacturing capacity is the binding constraint on the arrangement, and that the economic substance of the tolling relationship is that CPS supplies Brentwood Foods through Ridgeway acting as an intermediary or sales agent. The combined CPS + Ridgeway share is 21.0%. A critical observation warrants attention. The expert attributes the *full* \$140 million to CPS even though CPS manufactures only approximately 60% of the Brentwood Foods volume (approximately \$84 million in production value). CPS does not manufacture the remaining 40% (approximately \$56 million), which is produced by Ridgeway at its own facilities. Attributing the full \$140 million to CPS thus overcounts CPS's actual manufacturing involvement by approximately \$56 million. The FTC's rationale is that Ridgeway is the contractual seller of record. Ridgeway invoices Brentwood Foods, bears the commercial and credit risk on the customer relationship, manages all customer-facing functions, and reports this revenue in its financial statements and in its submissions to trade associations and the Census Bureau. The FTC's approach is consistent with the Census Bureau's NAICS 322212 data collection methodology, which attributes sales revenue to the entity that invoices the end customer, not to the entity that physically manufactures the product. This methodology is standard across virtually all government and trade association data sources used for market share estimation in the packaging industry. Under this attribution, the FTC's Market 2 results are as follows: CPS market share = 17.0% (\$2.41 billion / \$14.2 billion total market); Ridgeway market share = 4.0% (\$0.57 billion / \$14.2 billion total market). The combined CPS + Ridgeway share is 21.0%. The FTC's data sources — Census Bureau shipment data, subpoena responses from the top twenty industry participants, and Packaging Association of America annual reports — would all capture the \$140 million in Brentwood Foods revenue under Ridgeway, not CPS. Market Definition</u>** Market 4 is defined as all corrugated packaging products that meet the Food and Drug Administration's food-contact safety standards under 21 CFR Parts 174 through 178, sold in the United States. The relevant geographic market is the United States as a whole. This market is a sub-segment of Market 1 (corrugated packaging), defined by the regulatory and functional requirements applicable to packaging that comes into direct or indirect contact with food products. The basis for treating food-grade corrugated packaging as a separate relevant market rests on three principal considerations. **Supply-side substitutability barriers.** Manufacturing food-grade corrugated packaging requires the use of FDA-compliant raw materials, including food-safe inks, adhesives, coatings, and liner grades. Producers must maintain segregated production lines or implement rigorous changeover and cleaning protocols to prevent contamination. Quality assurance requirements include lot traceability systems, contamination testing regimens, and third-party certifications such as those administered by the Safe Quality Food Institute or the British Retail Consortium. FDA facility inspections add an additional layer of regulatory compliance. These requirements constitute meaningful barriers to supply-side substitution from non-food-grade corrugated producers, who would need to invest in equipment, raw material sourcing, employee training, certification processes, and quality systems before they could compete for food-grade contracts. **Demand-side characteristics.** Food and beverage companies require packaging that meets food-contact safety standards as a non-negotiable procurement criterion driven by federal regulation, brand protection, and consumer safety. These customers cannot substitute non-food-grade corrugated packaging. The price premium for food-grade corrugated products — typically 8% to 15% above standard corrugated — reflects the specialized manufacturing requirements and regulatory compliance costs. A hypothetical monopolist of food-grade corrugated packaging could profitably impose a small but significant and non-transitory increase in price because customers have no viable alternative. **Limited number of qualified suppliers.** Only a subset of corrugated packaging manufacturers maintain the certifications, raw material sourcing relationships, and production protocols necessary to serve the food-grade segment. This results in a more concentrated competitive structure than the broader corrugated market. The total market size for food-grade corrugated packaging in the United States was approximately \$9.4 billion in calendar year 2023. The Parties' expert, Dr. Brinkerhoff, does not define or address food-grade corrugated packaging as a separate relevant market in his expert report dated March 14, 2025. Unilateral Effects</u>** The concentration analysis presented in Sections IV through VII identifies two markets — Market 3 (Rigid Containers, Great Lakes Region) and Market 4 (Food-Grade Corrugated Packaging, National) — where the proposed merger triggers the structural presumption of illegality. Staff's preliminary assessment of competitive effects, summarized here, focuses on the potential for unilateral price increases in these markets. In Market 3, CPS and Ridgeway are the two largest firms in the eight-state Great Lakes region, holding 21.1% and 18.9% shares respectively. Their combined 40.0% share would create the dominant competitor in the market. Post-merger, the combined entity's nearest rival, Buckeye Plastics Inc. at 15.0%, would be less than half its size. The elimination of head-to-head competition between the number one and number two firms in the region creates strong economic incentives for unilateral price increases. When the merged firm considers raising prices on a particular product line or customer account, it can profitably do so to the extent that diverted sales flow to its own affiliate rather than to an independent competitor. The close competitive proximity of CPS and Ridgeway — both operating multiple facilities in the Great Lakes region and serving overlapping customer bases — suggests high diversion ratios between the two firms. In Market 4, the merger eliminates the fourth-largest competitor in food-grade corrugated packaging as an independent entity and creates the market leader. Ridgeway's 9.0% share in this segment, disproportionately high relative to its 3.2% overall corrugated share, reflects the significant competitive role it plays specifically in food-grade products. The elimination of this competitive force through merger with the second-largest firm creates incentives for unilateral price elevation in a segment where customers face high switching costs and limited qualified alternative suppliers. The FTC's use of delivered pricing captures the actual competitive conditions in these markets. Customers compare delivered prices when evaluating competing offers, and the relevant competitive interaction occurs at the customer level. FOB plant pricing may understate competitive proximity because it ignores freight equalization and zone pricing strategies commonly used by corrugated packaging manufacturers that effectively narrow geographic price differences among competitors. In Markets 1 and 2 (corrugated packaging and folding cartons nationally), unilateral effects concerns are less pronounced given the lower combined shares, the presence of multiple significant competitors, and the post-merger HHI levels that remain below the structural presumption threshold. **<u>VIII.B. Coordinated Effects</u>** The merger also raises concerns about coordinated effects, particularly in Market 4. Post-merger, the top two firms in food-grade corrugated packaging — the combined CPS-Ridgeway entity at 29.0% and Pinnacle Paper Group at 28.0% — would hold nearly symmetric market shares totaling 57.0%. Economic literature and agency experience indicate that markets with symmetric leading firms are more susceptible to tacit coordination, as the similar size and capabilities of the leading firms facilitate mutual recognition of interdependence and coordinated pricing behavior. In Market 3, the post-merger entity would be the clear market leader at 40.0%, a position that could facilitate price leadership. The next three competitors (Buckeye Plastics at 15.0%, Heartland Container at 11.1%, and Tri-State Molding at 8.1%) are individually too small to serve as effective competitive constraints on the merged entity and could find it more profitable to follow the leader's pricing rather than compete aggressively. More broadly, the corrugated packaging industry exhibits structural characteristics that facilitate coordination: substantially homogeneous products, relatively transparent pricing (many products are sold through competitive bidding processes that reveal market pricing), high barriers to entry due to capital-intensive manufacturing requirements, and repeated competitive interactions among incumbent firms across multiple customer accounts and geographic regions. **<u>VIII.C. Entry and Expansion</u>** Staff has assessed the likelihood that new entry or expansion by existing competitors could counteract the anticompetitive effects of the proposed merger within a timely period, defined as two years under the 2023 Merger Guidelines. Barriers to entry vary by market segment. In the corrugated packaging market (Market 1), barriers to entry are moderate. A greenfield corrugated manufacturing plant requires a capital investment of \$100 million or more, and the time required for construction, permitting, equipment installation, and customer qualification is typically eighteen to thirty-six months. Some available capacity among existing producers may allow incremental expansion, but large-scale entry sufficient to replace the competitive pressure of an independent Ridgeway is unlikely to occur within the relevant timeframe. In the folding carton market (Market 2), barriers are similar in nature and magnitude to those in corrugated packaging. In the rigid container market (Market 3, Great Lakes Region), barriers are high. Specialized injection molding and thermoforming equipment requires capital investment of \$15 million to \$50 million per facility. Customer qualification processes for rigid container suppliers typically take six to eighteen months. Additionally, the availability of suitable commercial real estate for new manufacturing facilities in the Great Lakes region is limited. These barriers make entry or expansion sufficient to counteract the merger's competitive effects unlikely to be timely, likely, and sufficient. In the food-grade corrugated market (Market 4), barriers are particularly high. In addition to the capital requirements common to all corrugated manufacturing, entrants into the food-grade segment must obtain FDA food-contact certifications, establish compliant supply chains for specialized raw materials, and undergo customer qualification processes that often take twelve to twenty-four months for food and beverage customers with rigorous quality and safety standards. The combination of capital, regulatory, and customer-qualification barriers makes timely and sufficient entry highly unlikely. **<u>IX.
